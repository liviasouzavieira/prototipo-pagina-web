The United Nations Security Council meets in New York on Friday for an emergency session convened after the intensification of fighting in the region over the past 72 hours. The meeting was jointly requested by two permanent members and is expected to last at least four hours, according to diplomatic sources interviewed for this story.

Before: "jointly requested by three permanent members"

Corrected on Apr 17 · 8:02 a.m. · flagged by World Desk Editor View all corrections in this coverage →

The agenda includes a proposed 72-hour humanitarian cease-fire, drafted from working papers that had circulated among diplomatic missions since the start of the week. According to The Outlet's reporting, the text prioritizes the opening of corridors for civilian evacuation and the delivery of aid.

Update (9:14 a.m.): the U.S. State Department said, in a statement released Friday morning, that Washington will back "any initiative that reduces violence against civilians" during the session. The statement followed an overnight meeting between the Secretary of State and the President.

The U.S. mission to the U.N. also confirmed that its permanent representative will speak during the debate phase, expected to begin in the early afternoon, New York time.

The cease-fire proposal faces resistance from at least two permanent members, who question the verification mechanisms laid out in the document. Analysts interviewed for this story consider it unlikely that the text will be approved in its current form.

In parallel, humanitarian organizations are warning of worsening conditions in areas affected by the bombings. According to estimates released on Thursday by the U.N. Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs, the number of internally displaced people has passed 420,000.

Mariana Ribeiro · international politics columnist
Column What the Security Council can — and can't — do

The U.N.'s legal architecture was designed for a bipolar world. The current conflict exposes the limits of a system that depends on unlikely consensus to function.

Published today · 7:40 a.m. · 8 min read

The legal architecture of the United Nations was designed in 1945, in the aftermath of the Second World War, for a world that had not yet crystallized into rival blocs — but that clearly needed a forum for coordination among the great powers of the time. The result was the Security Council as we know it: five permanent members (the U.S., the U.K., France, Russia and China) with veto power, and ten rotating members without it.

This design worked reasonably well in moments of cooperation between the powers — as in the Korea case in 1950, when the Soviet absence in protest allowed a resolution, or in the multilateral response to the invasion of Kuwait in 1990. But it failed loudly in others: Syria since 2011, Yemen, and now, possibly, Iran.

The veto structure creates a central paradox: the Council can only act with unanimity among the five permanents, and unanimity presupposes an alignment that, in practice, rarely exists. When the interests of the permanents diverge, the result is paralysis — not by procedural failure, but by design.

What, then, can the Council still do? It can pass non-binding resolutions that serve as symbolic and diplomatic pressure. It can authorize peacekeeping operations when there is consensus, as happened in Lebanon in 2006. It can send neutral observers, as may occur with the current proposal on Iran. And it can issue condemnations that, although without immediate legal teeth, leave footprints in the international record.

What it cannot do, without consensus among the permanents, is anything with binding force under Chapter VII of the Charter — enforceable sanctions, military intervention, asset freezes. This means that, in the current case, even if the cease-fire proposal is approved with 12 or 13 votes in favor, it will only become an obligation if none of the five permanents vetoes. And the diplomatic calculation about who is willing to veto what is precisely what we are watching unfold this Friday.

The uncomfortable truth is that the Security Council, in its current form, is more useful as a signal of tensions than as an instrument for resolving them. Reforming it — including, for example, a sixth permanent seat or the abolition of the veto in cases of mass human rights violations — is an old and stuck debate. While the deadlock holds, crises like Iran's expose with brutality what we already knew: the multilateral system was built for another world, and is being asked to operate in this one.
